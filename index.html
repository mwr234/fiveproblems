Problem 01The 3n + 1 conjectureOpen since 1937

Even? Halve it.  Odd? Triple it and add one.

The 3n + 1 conjecture Whatever number you start with, the rule always brings you down to 1.

Also called the Collatz conjecture. A distributed computing project has checked every start up to 271, about 2.4 sextillion numbers. All of them reach 1. This proves nothing. Infinitely many numbers are left over, so the fraction checked is still zero.

Problem 02The inscribed square problemOpen since 1911

Draw a loop. Find a square in it.

Drag inside the box to draw a closed loop.

Any closed loop that doesn't cross itself. Wobbly, lumpy, ugly — doesn't matter. Four of its points make a perfect square.

The inscribed square conjecture Every closed loop you can draw holds a square.
Problem 02The inscribed square problem

It works every time.
That isn't a proof.

Otto Toeplitz asked this in 1911. It has been proved for smooth curves, for convex curves, for curves with corners, for fractal curves — for basically every class anyone has been able to pin down.

The general case is still open, and the obstacle is exactly the curves you can't draw: continuous loops so pathological they have no tangent anywhere, infinite length in a finite space, wiggling at every scale. Those are the ones nobody can rule out.

Problem 03The lonely runner conjectureOpen since 1967

They start together and never stop. Each one has to get his own clear moment, and they do not come at the same time. Keep going until every runner has had one.

The lonely runner conjecture With 3 runners, every one of them eventually gets 1/3 of a lap of clear road on both sides.
Problem 03The lonely runner conjecture

Proved for ten runners.
Open for eleven.

ProvedUnknown
2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 runners
11 or more

Sixty years of work has gotten this from three runners to ten. Each new case has taken years and, lately, supercomputer time.

Most of that progress is from the last twelve months.

Seven runners fell in 2008, then nothing for seventeen years. In September 2025 Matthieu Rosenfeld got eight with a computer search. Weeks later nine and ten fell to Tanupat Trakulthongchai, an Oxford undergraduate, who found a way to sieve the search down to a feasible size.

He did it by improving one step of someone else's search, not by inventing anything new.

Problem 04The twin prime conjectureOpen since antiquity

Primes two apart. Find the next pair.

The twin prime conjecture There are infinitely many twin primes.
Problem 04The twin prime conjecture

They thin out. They never stop.

Proved: some gap this size recurs for everWanted
gap ≤ 246
gap = 2

In 2013 nobody could prove that any fixed gap repeats for ever. Yitang Zhang got it below 70 million; a Polymath collaboration with James Maynard drove it to 246 within a year. It has not moved since.

Problem 05Goldbach’s conjectureOpen since 1742

Every even number. Two primes. Add them.

Goldbach’s conjecture Every even number above 2 is two primes added together.
Problem 05Goldbach’s conjecture

Not one way. Many ways.

Checked by computerEvery larger number
4 → 4×1018
unknown

Every even number up to 4×1018 has been checked and every one works. The odd-number version — every odd number above 7 is a sum of three primes — was proved by Harald Helfgott in 2013. The even version, needing only two primes, is still open.

In closing

None of this is settled history.

Look at who moved them recently.

Yitang Zhang was an adjunct lecturer nobody had heard of. He put the first finite bound on prime gaps in 2013.

Tanupat Trakulthongchai was an undergraduate. He advanced the lonely runner conjecture in 2025.

Harald Helfgott settled the odd-number half of Goldbach in 2013, after 271 years. The even half is still open.

None of them needed a new branch of mathematics. They needed one good idea about a problem you can state in a sentence.
